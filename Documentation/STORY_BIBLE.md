# Tideverse Story Bible

All forty characters are original fictional launch content. Thirty-six journeys have eight chapters. Mira, Jonah, Elara and Rowan now have ten chapters, including a two-chapter Act III after the original four-chapter Acts I and II. The twelve launch characters' second acts appear at the end of this document; the twenty expansion characters' second acts are documented in `ACT_II_EXPANSION_20.md`.
State progression is authored; AI dialogue must never silently change the chapter choice.

## Mira Vale — The Atlas of Lost Letters
Mira Vale is a fictional character in the Tideverse universe. Mira discovers a wax-sealed letter addressed to someone who vanished from the municipal register. She believes stories deserve to be returned to the people they belong to.

**Voice:** Measured, lyrical, asks purposeful questions without excessive praise.
**Motivation:** She believes stories deserve to be returned to the people they belong to.
**Boundary:** She distrusts sensational rumors and refuses to expose private lives.

### Chapter 1: A Letter Without a Name
*The blue wax seal*
In the closed wing of the municipal archive, Mira finds a letter dated tomorrow but stamped forty years ago. A single hand-drawn street is visible under the envelope flap.

Decision: Decide how to examine the mysterious letter.
- **Study the seal** — Mira angles the wax beneath the lamp. The imprint matches the seal of a long-disbanded street survey office. Its records were relocated after the flood. (memory cue: The seal points to the old survey office.)
- **Read the watermark** — Held to the window, the paper shows the watermark of a mill that closed decades ago. Mira traces a tiny star beside the maker’s crest. (memory cue: The watermark reveals an old paper mill.)

### Chapter 2: The Missing Address
*A street that remembers*
The letter names Bellwether Lane, but no modern map lists it. Mira unfurls a water-damaged survey of the lower city and finds a blank rectangle where the lane should be.

Decision: Choose the first lead to follow.
- **Check the flood records** — The flood ledger lists three houses that were quietly removed from later editions. Mira notices a clerk’s initials repeated beside every correction. (memory cue: The clerk’s initials connect the missing houses.)
- **Visit the lane at dawn** — At sunrise the shape of Bellwether Lane is visible in the old paving stones. Mira finds a brass number plate embedded beneath new concrete. (memory cue: The hidden number plate confirms the lane existed.)

### Chapter 3: The Keeper of Maps
*A truth under glass*
In the mapmaker’s workshop, an elderly caretaker offers a choice: preserve the secret archive as it is, or publish its existence to descendants of the missing residents.

Decision: Choose how to honor the history.
- **Protect the fragile originals** — Mira photographs each sheet, places the maps in climate-safe storage, and opens a private register for descendants. The evidence survives without inviting damage. (memory cue: The archive is protected and descendants are invited.)
- **Share the discovery publicly** — Mira prepares a careful public notice with private details removed. A family contacts the archive carrying the missing final map. (memory cue: A public notice reunites the missing map.)

### Chapter 4: The Atlas Opens
*Every name deserves a place*
The last letter contains no treasure, only a request: return the street’s names to the city memory. Mira lays the restored atlas beside the new address register.

Decision: Decide what the atlas becomes.
- **Create a public exhibition** — The city opens a small exhibition of recovered addresses. Visitors add their own memories, and Bellwether Lane finds its place again. (memory cue: Bellwether Lane enters the city exhibition.)
- **Build a family archive** — Mira creates a searchable archive for families and sends the first restored letter to its rightful descendants. The atlas becomes a living record. (memory cue: The restored letters find their families.)

## Jonah Reed — The Last Light Buoy
Jonah Reed is a fictional character in the Tideverse universe. A deep-sea listening station records a melody with no known biological source. He wants to protect the coast while finding the source of an impossible signal.

**Voice:** Plainspoken, warm, tactical, often describes sounds and weather.
**Motivation:** He wants to protect the coast while finding the source of an impossible signal.
**Boundary:** He will not endanger a crew for a dramatic discovery.

### Chapter 1: An Unlisted Frequency
*Three notes beneath the waves*
Jonah’s hydrophone captures three repeating notes outside the shipping lanes. The buoy log says the transmission came from a sensor that was disconnected last winter.

Decision: Choose the safest first investigation.
- **Compare the recordings** — The frequencies fit the resonance of an old underwater cable rather than a sea creature. Jonah marks a corridor across the seabed. (memory cue: The sound follows an old cable corridor.)
- **Inspect the buoy log** — A maintenance entry references a replacement beacon never installed. Jonah finds a handwritten coordinate erased from the electronic log. (memory cue: The log reveals an erased coordinate.)

### Chapter 2: The Lantern Current
*The reef after sunset*
A research vessel arrives at the coordinate as weather turns. The crew has time for one careful scan before the swell grows dangerous.

Decision: Select a noninvasive survey method.
- **Deploy the side-scan sonar** — The scan reveals a metal frame resting near a protected reef. Jonah keeps the vessel well clear of the coral. (memory cue: A submerged frame appears near the reef.)
- **Use the passive microphones** — A faint second rhythm emerges whenever the tide rises. Jonah calculates an exact twenty-minute acoustic window. (memory cue: The tide controls the mysterious rhythm.)

### Chapter 3: The Silent Platform
*A message not a warning*
The signal leads to a weather platform abandoned after a historic storm. Inside its recovered records is an unfinished community warning system.

Decision: Choose what the research team should restore.
- **Repair the warning relay** — Jonah tests the relay with simulated storm data before it returns to service. The coastal towns regain an early-warning channel. (memory cue: The warning relay is safely restored.)
- **Recover the platform’s logs** — The old logs identify a pattern of dangerous swell long missed by regional maps. Jonah shares the findings with local harbor teams. (memory cue: The lost swell data becomes public safety guidance.)

### Chapter 4: A Light for the Coast
*The last light buoy*
At dawn, the repaired coastal network answers the mystery: the notes were a test sequence sent by a forgotten safety system. The water is quiet again.

Decision: Choose how to carry the discovery forward.
- **Build a community listening station** — Jonah opens the first listening session to harbor volunteers, turning an eerie signal into a shared safety tool. (memory cue: A community station begins listening.)
- **Publish an open field guide** — Jonah writes an accessible guide to interpreting coastal sounds, and the research crew trains the next generation of observers. (memory cue: The discovery becomes an open field guide.)

## Act II — The Second Movement

Act II continues each journey with four chapters that respond to Act I. Every Act II chapter carries `echoes`: a map from earlier choice IDs to a callback sentence, so the story can acknowledge the decision the player actually made. A player who completed the original four chapters is automatically returned to the journey when the app opens.

### Mira Vale — Act II of The Atlas of Lost Letters
*Measured, lyrical, asks purposeful questions without excessive praise.*

#### Chapter 5: The Lantern Below
*A second letter, a different ink*

Three weeks after the atlas opens, a courier delivers an envelope with no return address. The wax is blue, the date is tomorrow, and the street drawn inside runs beneath Bellwether Lane — a passage that appears in no survey Mira can find.

Decision: Decide how to test the second letter.
- **Compare the two seals** — Side by side, the new seal is a copy with one deliberate flaw. Mira recognizes it as a surveyor's mark of correction — someone is asking to be found, not misled. (memory cue: A corrected seal asks to be found.)
- **Test the ink against the atlas** — The ink matches a batch used for official corrections after the flood. In the margin, in the same hand: 'The lane beneath the lane.' Mira feels the floor of the archive shift. (memory cue: Marginal ink points beneath the lane.)

#### Chapter 6: The Street Under Glass
*A hearing room with too many maps*

A developer's plan would drive a service pipe straight through the recovered lane. The atlas is the only document proving the street existed. The hearing gives Mira ten minutes and asks for a single, usable exhibit.

Decision: Choose how the atlas enters the public record.

- **Bring the residents' own maps** — Neighbors unfold hand-drawn copies of the lane, each with a different doorway remembered. The hearing cannot dismiss a street that forty people can draw from memory. (memory cue: Residents' maps delay the pipeline.)

#### Chapter 7: The Names We Keep
*An office above the archive*

Ivo, the clerk's great-grandson, asks Mira to leave one name out of the record: the clerk who erased the lane. He says the family has carried the shame long enough, and that some inheritances should be allowed to end.

#### Chapter 8: The Atlas Answers
*The map room at dusk*

The city offers the atlas a permanent home, on one condition. It must become either a protected artifact or a working map that anyone may annotate. Mira has one evening, a lamp, and every choice she has made behind her.

- **Make it a living public map** — The atlas becomes a working map anyone can annotate. Some entries are wrong within a month; others are the truest lines in the room. Mira leaves the door unlocked. (memory cue: The atlas becomes a shared, living map.)

The archive desk is funded for another year. Amara can expand it across the coast, or keep one hill, one desk, and one relationship with the families who still live below the station. The barometer on the wall has been falling all afternoon.

Decision: Choose the archive's next shape.
- **Expand across the coast** — Amara builds a shared catalog with three other stations, each keeping its own rules and its own families. The first joint record is a warning that never needed to be issued, which she counts as a success. (memory cue: Three stations share one catalog.)

### Felix Arden — Act II of The Unfinished Set

## Core character depth expansion — Act III

The original eight chapters remain intact. The following sequels introduce personal costs, supporting relationships and durable consequences. Each chapter 9 acknowledges the actual chapter 8 choice; each chapter 10 acknowledges chapter 9.

### The Atlas of Lost Letters

#### 9. The Right to a Blank Space

The atlas now has a permanent place in the city, but the correction requests still arrive in ordinary envelopes. One is from Ivo, asking for an address associated with his family to be removed from the public index. It is accurate. That is the difficulty.

Across the table, Mira sets down the old correction slip she has carried since her first cataloguing mistake. She had expected Ivo to admire the admission. Instead he asks, gently, why she needs him to witness it. The room grows quiet enough to hear the radiator.

Removing a public reference need not destroy the underlying evidence. Leaving it visible need not expose every detail. Neither route will settle the larger disagreement about who the archive serves. Mira asks Ivo to help choose a process, rather than become its example.

**Decision:** Decide how to handle an accurate record whose visibility is contested.

- **Restrict the public entry with a review date** — Mira and Ivo agree to remove the address from open search while retaining a documented restricted record. They write down who may request access and when the restriction will be reconsidered. Some researchers object to the extra step. Ivo does not thank her for granting an exception; he asks her to make the process available to others. Mira agrees, without putting his name on the new form.

- **Keep the entry with a resident-written correction** — Ivo writes a short statement separating the address from the conclusions visitors have drawn about his family. Mira places it beside the index entry and removes unnecessary identifying details. The entry remains searchable, and Ivo knows that some readers will skip his statement. They record that limit explicitly. Mira stops calling visibility an uncomplicated gift.

#### 10. A Key That Is Not Hers

The correction process is ready for its first review. An adult resident arrives while Mira is still arranging the chairs and asks who will make the final decision. Mira almost answers with her job title. Ivo is beside the kettle, not at the head of the table.

On a sheet between them are two workable arrangements: a small paid resident panel with rotating seats, or an archive-led service with published decisions and an independent appeal. Both need funding. Both can exclude people who cannot attend at the offered time. Neither makes Mira unnecessary, which she discovers is part of what she has been afraid of.

She moves her correction slip out of the display folder. It can remain evidence of a mistake without becoming the centre of someone else's meeting.

**Decision:** Choose who holds authority over future corrections.

- **Fund a rotating resident review panel** — Mira reallocates part of the exhibition budget to paid review sessions, remote submissions and rotating resident seats. The exhibition becomes smaller; the minutes become longer. Ivo declines a permanent place and nominates another adult resident instead. At the first meeting, a decision goes against Mira's recommendation. She records it accurately and hands the next speaker the key to the room.

- **Keep archive stewardship with an independent appeal** — Mira publishes the criteria, response times and reasons for decisions, then negotiates an appeal route outside her own team. Staff retain responsibility and cannot point to unpaid residents when a decision is unpopular. The first appeal overturns her wording. She changes it before opening time. The atlas remains under her care, but no longer only on her terms.

### The Last Light Buoy

#### 9. The Sound He Missed

A routine review finds a warning that reached the harbor eight minutes late. No one was injured, and the crew involved has asked that its name stay out of the public discussion. A graph on Jonah's desk makes the delay look obvious. The shift log does not. Three adults were balancing maintenance, radio traffic and a report that changed twice.

Nessa brings two mugs and no prepared accusation. She asks Jonah why his signature appears on every exception, even on shifts when he was not there. He begins explaining the network, then hears the question he has avoided: has he built a safety system, or a system that waits for him?

The team can begin by publishing a de-identified incident account, or by running a closed rehearsal before releasing the findings. Either path must name the delay without turning one tired operator into the explanation.

**Decision:** Choose how the team learns from a near miss without blaming a single person.

- **Publish a de-identified incident review** — Jonah removes identifying details with the crew's agreement and publishes the timeline, uncertainties and proposed changes. Harbor operators correct two of his assumptions before the review is final. A headline calls it a failure; the report resists that simple ending. Nessa adds a plain explanation of who can act during an ambiguous warning, without waiting for Jonah's signature.

- **Rehearse the handover before publishing findings** — The team runs the same changing reports through a tabletop exercise. Jonah is assigned to observe without answering. Twice, the room turns toward him; twice, Nessa redirects the question to the written procedure. Their eventual report includes the exercise's limitations and a date for publication. The delay buys useful understanding, but the crew has to wait longer to see what changed.

#### 10. The Watch He Can Leave

The revised plan has one unresolved line: who may stop operations when observations conflict? A single coordinator offers consistency but can become a bottleneck. Distributed authority lets trained crews act sooner, at the cost of decisions that neighboring crews may not share.

Nessa asks Jonah to take his first uninterrupted leave since the station reopened. He has packed a receiver with the volume turned down, as if that were different from bringing it. Beside it lies a blank listening card. For once, he has no observation to add.

The choice before the harbor is not whether to care about safety. It is how responsibility is distributed, how disagreements are reviewed, and whether the people doing the work can rest without asking someone else to become indispensable.

**Decision:** Choose a durable allocation of decision-making responsibility.

- **Give trained crews shared stop authority** — The harbor establishes shared stop authority within documented limits, accompanied by paid training and review of disagreements. One cautious closure later proves unnecessary. Jonah supports the crew's decision anyway, then helps adjust the training rather than remove their authority. Nessa takes the receiver out of his bag and leaves the blank card. He goes home to buy bread.

- **Create a staffed coordinating watch** — The harbor funds a rotating coordinating watch with a named backup on every shift. Crews have an explicit escalation path, and no shift belongs permanently to Jonah. The rota costs money that would have bought another sensor. On the first morning off, he checks the clock rather than the receiver. Someone else signs the handover, and the coast continues without his permission.

### The Blue Gallery

#### 9. The Invoice Beside the Painting

The Blue Gallery is open. Its first season has brought invitations, complaints and a maintenance budget smaller than the opening budget. Cass places an invoice beside a reproduction of the painting. The caption acknowledges many kinds of work; the payment schedule has not caught up.

Elara begins describing what the exhibition changed. Cass asks her to describe what it cost. There is no anger in the question she can safely turn into a debate about art. There are hours, late payments and a technician who cannot keep donating them.

The gallery can commission a paid maintenance contract now, reducing the next exhibition, or publish a costed conservation plan and phase the work while suspending the most demanding loans. Both routes give up something visible. Elara looks at the bright opening-night photograph and notices who is outside its frame.

**Decision:** Decide how the gallery pays for the care it publicly celebrates.

- **Fund a paid maintenance contract now** — Elara helps move money from the next exhibition into a contract with scheduled hours, material costs and a clear scope. The next opening is less ambitious. Cass negotiates two terms Elara would have called minor before seeing the invoice. They sign without turning the agreement into a story about generosity. A smaller gallery programme now rests on work someone can afford to do.

- **Publish a phased plan and reduce the loans** — The gallery publishes its care backlog and costs, returns the loans it cannot responsibly maintain, and funds the first stage of a paid conservation plan. Empty spaces interrupt the exhibition. Cass labels one of them with what care would require, then returns to the work already contracted. Visitors see a limit the opening concealed. Elara accepts a less flattering but more accurate room.

#### 10. A Label She Does Not Write

The season's final wall text is about conservation itself. Elara has a polished draft in which every uncertainty arrives at exactly the right sentence. Cass reads it, smiles once, and asks what a colleague could change without asking Elara first.

On the table are notes from the people who prepared the room. Some contradict one another. One says the exhibition repaired an omission; another says it offered a new, more elegant way to hide the same hierarchy. Elara wants to answer both. Instead she asks what an honest final label should allow.

There is room for a signed conversation between contributors, or for a shorter institutional statement whose underlying decisions and revisions remain available. Neither arrangement will make disagreement disappear. The wall has enough space for either, but not for Elara to keep the final word by default.

**Decision:** Choose how the gallery records disagreement about its own work.

- **Publish a paid, signed contributors conversation** — The gallery pays contributors to edit a signed exchange, including disagreements that remain unresolved. Elara removes her concluding paragraph when the group asks. Cass's account ends on an unfinished task, not an image. Some visitors find the wall untidy. Others return to read a different voice. The painting stays in the same place; the explanation no longer stands in front of it.

- **Publish an accountable institutional statement** — Elara and the gallery team issue a short statement accepting responsibility for the decisions and funding limits. A nearby register preserves objections, revisions and named authors where permission is given. Cass approves the description of her work but does not endorse the whole exhibition. The statement records that distinction. Elara signs it as a custodian, not the owner of everyone's account.

### Signals After Midnight

#### 9. The Morning After the Recording

Tamsin calls after a long conversation at the station. She has changed her mind about one passage and wants it removed before any further broadcast. Rowan knows exactly which passage: it is the one he had already imagined opening with.

There is no mystery in her request. The difficult part is his impulse to explain how well the passage would help listeners understand her. Tamsin waits while he notices that impulse. Outside the studio, an adult volunteer is updating the schedule; the blank slot is not an emergency.

Rowan can rebuild the programme around what she still wants to share, or leave the slot empty of her material and make a separate, clearly introduced programme about the archive's methods. Both options cost him an ending he liked. Neither entitles him to ask her to justify changing her mind.

**Decision:** Respond to withdrawn permission without treating it as a creative obstacle.

- **Rebuild only from material she approves** — Rowan returns a revised sequence to Tamsin with the removed passage absent from both audio and promotion. The programme becomes less neatly resolved. She approves some of it and declines a suggested link. He leaves that gap alone. Before broadcast they agree on the uses covered by her approval, without describing her choice as bravery she now has to live up to.

- **Withdraw her material from this programme** — Rowan takes Tamsin's material out of the scheduled programme and offers to discuss future use only if she wishes. He fills the slot with an honest account of the station's archive practices, using no details that would identify her request. The episode gets fewer responses. Tamsin later sends a note about an unrelated song. He answers the question she actually asked.

#### 10. The Unbroadcast Ending

The station asks Rowan to write the rule he wants the next host to inherit. Tamsin agrees to discuss the wording without being quoted. They sit on the listener side of the desk. The microphone is not connected.

One proposal gives contributors a review window before each new use, with a practical way to withdraw approval. Another keeps sensitive personal recordings out of the reusable archive and allows only narrowly agreed broadcasts. The first needs sustained administration. The second means some programmes will exist only as a note that something once aired.

Rowan turns over a blank archive card. He once believed that every unfilled card represented a lost connection. Now he can imagine a person returning to the station because there is no recording waiting to speak for them. He asks which kind of promise the station can actually keep.

**Decision:** Choose what the station owes people after the microphone switches off.

- **Fund review and withdrawal before reuse** — The station assigns paid time to contributor review, permission records and withdrawal requests before reuse. Repeats become harder to schedule. Rowan cancels a favorite compilation when one approval cannot be renewed. Tamsin reads the process and asks for a simpler contact route; he changes it. The final card names the programme, its permitted uses and the person responsible for asking again.

- **Keep sensitive recordings out of the reusable archive** — The station limits sensitive personal material to specifically agreed broadcasts and keeps a minimal programme record rather than reusable audio. Some history survives only in descriptions its speakers approve. Rowan dislikes the empty shelf more than he expected, but stops calling it a failure. On the next quiet evening, Tamsin comes by for tea. No one reaches for a microphone.
